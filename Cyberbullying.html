Going viral: Investigating the short- and long-term traumatic effects of cyberbullying victimization on adolescents in schools

Adapted from Keith, S., Scheuerman, H. L. & Smith, A. L. (2025), 'Going viral: Investigating the short- and long-term traumatic effects of cyberbullying victimization on adolescents in schools', Psychological Trauma: Theory, Research, Practice, and Policy, 17(Suppl 1), S51–S59.

A   The main and somewhat surprising result is that cyberbullying's damaging effects show up mostly in the short term — the same year it happens — and largely fade after two years. The researchers expected the harm to persist or even deepen over time, but for most outcomes it did not. This challenges a common assumption in the field that because cyberbullying can circulate endlessly online, its effects should be especially long-lasting.

B   The authors used a large, nationally representative U.S. survey of students aged 12 to 18, drawing on two waves (2011 and 2013). This let them compare short-term effects (measuring outcomes in the same year as the bullying) against longer-term effects (measuring 2013 outcomes based on bullying that happened back in 2011). The final sample was 912 students. They looked at three kinds of outcomes: fear at school, school difficulties (avoiding places, skipping class, and grades), and antisocial behavior (fighting and carrying weapons). A key strength is that they used data over time rather than a single snapshot, which most earlier studies relied on.

C   Perhaps the study's central contribution is showing that cyberbullying on its own was linked to relatively few bad outcomes. What drove most of the harm was experiencing both cyberbullying and traditional (in-person) bullying at the same time. Students in this 'both' group were the ones most likely to be afraid, avoid places, skip class, get lower grades, and fight. This matters because victims of cyberbullying very often experience traditional bullying too — the two overlap heavily — so cyberbullying rarely happens in isolation. In the short term, the students facing both forms were dramatically more likely to be fearful, to avoid places or activities, and to get into fights than students who weren't victimized at all.

D   Even so, cyberbullying by itself was not harmless. It had a distinctive, standalone link to two things in particular: lower grades and increased fighting. These showed up both in the short term and — unusually — as lasting effects two years later. Students cyberbullied in 2011 still tended to have lower grades and a higher likelihood of fighting in 2013. The authors suggest grades may be especially vulnerable because students often do schoolwork on the same laptops, tablets, and phones where the bullying happens, so the distress follows them right into their studying.

E   The proposed chain of cause and effect runs like this: online victimization produces intense negative emotions (fear, anger, sadness, embarrassment), and because it can follow a young person everywhere, is public, and can resurface through screenshots, there is often 'no escape.' These emotions then undermine a teenager's ability to cope, which spills over into behavior — some withdraw and avoid (skipping class, staying away from places), while others lash out to try to regain a sense of control (fighting). Adolescents' still-developing capacity for self-regulation makes them especially susceptible to this emotional-to-behavioral pathway.

F   Contrary to expectations, no form of bullying predicted carrying weapons to school. The authors are careful here, suggesting this might be a measurement issue rather than a real absence of risk — their survey asked about carrying a weapon 'for protection,' and a student might carry one for other reasons (to threaten others, or for bravado) that the question wouldn't capture.

G   The authors float a thoughtful explanation for why long-term effects were weaker: students who endure ongoing bullying may develop resilience over time. They're careful to note they can't confirm this, partly because they had no data on what happened in the in-between year (2012) — a student counted as 'bullied in 2011' might have had a bullying-free stretch afterward, which could explain the recovery.

H   The findings come with real caveats. The group experiencing cyberbullying only was very small (just 11 students in 2011 and 15 in 2013), which limits how confidently those specific results generalize. The survey also asked only whether bullying happened 'in the past 6 months,' so there's no way to know how recent, frequent, or severe it was — and the authors stress that recency, frequency, and intensity likely matter a great deal. They also couldn't measure the full range of possible harms (such as substance use, self-harm, or depression), so the picture is partial.

I   The practical message is that early identification and adult support matter. Because most harm is concentrated in the short term, catching it early can prevent it from taking root. Their recommendations center on schools building trusting relationships with students and families to spot victims sooner; creating cyberbullying-specific programs (distinct from traditional anti-bullying efforts) that teach responsible and respectful online behavior; involving parents through workshops and better reporting channels; and offering mentorship and counseling so victims have a safe adult to turn to. They also note that limiting personal device use during the school day, and parents delaying and monitoring their children's early technology use, may help.

J   Cyberbullying is genuinely traumatic and uniquely harms school performance and raises the risk of fighting — but its worst effects tend to hit immediately rather than lingering for years, and the heaviest damage falls on students who are bullied both online and in person. The window for support is early, which makes prompt detection by teachers and other adults the key lever for reducing harm.
